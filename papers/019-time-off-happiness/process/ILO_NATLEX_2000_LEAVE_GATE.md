# ARIS4C019 · ILO/NATLEX 2000 annual-leave legal snapshot gate

**Recovered:** 2026-09-24  
**Source:** ILO Governing Body GB.279/LILS/WP/PRS/1/2, Annex I.  
**Frame:** 41 member States that had not ratified C132 and whose annual-leave legislation had been adopted or amended during the preceding ten years.

## Why this matters

This is a genuine late-20th-century national-law layer. The ILO states that it used NATLEX and a few supplementary sources, focused on the **absolute minimum** annual-leave entitlement in laws of general application, and excluded special employee/contract categories. The information was not directly validated with governments, so the ILO itself treats the conclusions as indicative. citeturn480880search12

## Coverage

- countries: **41**
- ILO duration classification:
  - at least three weeks: **25**
  - below three weeks: **15**
  - not specified in the examined general law: **1**
- source-reported countries above the C132 three-week minimum: **17**
- approximate five-day harmonization:
  - high confidence: **19**
  - medium confidence: **13**
  - deliberately not harmonized: **9**

The source itself reports 25/41 countries at three-to-five weeks, 17 above the Convention minimum, 15 below it, and China with no minimum specified in the general law examined. citeturn749005view0

## Canonical-vs-derived rule

`raw_minimum_entitlement` is canonical.

`approx_5day_workweek_days` is only a screening variable:
- calendar/consecutive weeks are converted using five working days per week;
- explicit six-day working-week values are multiplied by 5/6;
- monthly working-day accruals are annualized and, where the weekly basis is unstated, use the later ILO/TRAVAIL six-day-default convention;
- ambiguous "days", month definitions, or work-accrual denominators are left missing rather than guessed.

No candidate legal reform is accepted solely from this approximate field.

## Scope caveat

The 41-country frame is **selected**, not globally representative: countries had to be non-ratifiers of C132 and have relatively recent legislation available to the ILO. Use it for historical reconstruction and within-country cross-time screening, not population-level prevalence estimates for all countries in 2000.
